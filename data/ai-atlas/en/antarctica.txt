A radar image from Sentinel-1 shows a giant iceberg among sea ice, smaller fragments and the coast. Drawing a precise boundary by hand takes a researcher a few minutes. In a study published in 2023, a neural network produced an outline in less than a hundredth of a second. The authors assessed its performance on 191 images. When an iceberg was larger than those the model had learned from, it sometimes missed part of the object. [1]

In the foreground is an image that researchers can analyse much faster. Behind it is a continent undergoing changes with consequences far beyond the next satellite pass. NASA estimates that the Antarctic ice sheet lost about 135 billion tonnes of ice a year on average from 2002 to 2025. That loss contributed to global mean sea level rise. For a research team, this is a change in mass. For a person living on a low-lying coast, it is part of understanding how high the water may reach in a future flood. [2]

## What the outline tells us

An iceberg is a floating piece that has broken away from a glacier or ice shelf. Calving is a natural process. Researchers studying a warming climate also need to know what is happening to the ice resting on land and how fast it is moving into the ocean. Tracing an iceberg on a radar image helps measure its area. NASA’s estimates of the total change in mass across Antarctica draw on other measurements from the GRACE and GRACE Follow-On satellite missions. [1][2][3]

These observations answer complementary questions. Measuring the size and path of a large iceberg helps scientists study how it breaks up and releases fresh water. Changes in the mass of the ice sheet reveal something difficult to see in a single picture. Ice shelves also matter. These floating extensions can hold back ice behind them. If a shelf weakens, glaciers feeding it from land can speed up. Nearby glaciers did so after the Larsen B Ice Shelf disintegrated in 2002. [1][3]

A model helps with the first job by processing more images in less time. It can give researchers more time to decide which cases to examine closely. In the study, the median absolute difference from the hand-drawn area, expressed as a percentage, was 4.1 per cent. That is a result for the images in that test. An image at a new scale, a boundary that is harder to see or an object bigger than those in the training data may call for a further check. Unusual events, which deserve the closest attention, are precisely where that can matter. [1]

The speed also makes it possible to go back to images that might otherwise have been set aside and look more closely at what happened between major events. Comparability matters. If a team changes models halfway through a series, someone needs to check whether a jump comes from the ice or from the new tool. A set of images assessed by people provides a reference for finding out where a model improved a measurement and where it changed the way a feature was read.

NASA estimates that Antarctic ice loss in this period contributed about 0.4 millimetres a year to the rise in global mean sea level. That small mark on a ruler represents one contribution spread across the oceans. Other sources of sea level rise add to it. Local conditions and tides matter to an individual port or house. An observation first made far away can help people who may never see Antarctica, once it is combined with the data they need to make decisions on their own coasts. [2]

## The electricity is outside the frame

A satellite image reveals little about the infrastructure that makes it possible to carry out more analysis with AI. The International Energy Agency put electricity consumption by data centres worldwide at about 485 terawatt-hours in 2025. Its central projection reaches roughly 950 terawatt-hours in 2030. Those figures cover all data centres. In the same projection, electricity use by AI-focused data centres grows faster. [4]

A great range of tasks sits inside the total. One is the analysis of radar imagery. Others include training general-purpose models and running them for large numbers of people. The total shows the scale of the infrastructure. Assessing the cost of a particular job takes a separate measurement. Comparing two ways of analysing ice means looking at the number of images, the accuracy the researchers need and whether the system has to be trained from scratch or can use a model already built. [1][4]

Emissions depend on where the power comes from. If extra demand requires more gas or coal to be burnt, the benefit of the calculation may be felt far from some of its environmental costs. Where cleaner electricity is available, the balance can be different. Where and when non-urgent computations run can therefore matter. The person looking at an image or a chatbot’s reply rarely sees what the electricity system had to bring online to do the work. [4]

For a scientist or an institution choosing a method, energy use across the whole process would be valuable information. The time it takes to draw an outline measures one stage. A team can use a model thousands of times, with some costs already incurred when it was taught to recognise the ice. A useful comparison would examine the energy needed to check the same set of images to a similar level of quality. It would show how much work was removed and how much of it shifted to another place and time.

## What we do with a better view

An observation becomes valuable if it allows someone to ask a better question. Is an apparent change in the rate of an iceberg’s break-up worth a closer look? Can the data show where its fresh water went? Can a series of measurements help distinguish a short-lived change from one going on for years? Somebody still needs to choose the right measure and take responsibility for the conclusion.

Energy data makes it possible to ask similar questions of the technology. With comparable results, it becomes easier to judge which models contribute new knowledge and what it took to produce it. Sometimes it makes sense to observe an area nobody has looked at closely. Sometimes a team can use measurements already available instead of running the same task again. The cost of a calculation is often spread across many places and hours, which is a reason to measure it carefully.

A scientist can get an outline in a fraction of a second. Making useful knowledge from it still takes time spent comparing images and finding out what changed. Behind the next image there will also be decisions about where the electricity came from and what calculations were worth doing. The boundary of the ice deserves close attention. So does the work happening beyond the edge of the picture.
