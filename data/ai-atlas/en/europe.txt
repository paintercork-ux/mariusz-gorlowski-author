An application system operated by iTutorGroup automatically rejected some people applying to become tutors in 2020, according to the US Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. The agency said the programme screened out women aged 55 or over and men aged 60 or over. The case ended in 2023 in a settlement providing $365,000 for affected applicants. The software was making one decision after another. Each person turned away had only their own application to go on. [1]

That case makes a risk addressed by Europe’s AI Act easier to picture. An age cut-off can be found in the rules of a programme. When a tool learns from past recruitment decisions, it may pick up on less obvious details. Think of an algorithm that responds to career length, the year somebody qualified or the language used in a CV. A form can leave out a question about age, and its answer may still depend on details that tend to go with it.

## Who reads the documentation

The European framework takes seriously the possibility that an AI system affects somebody’s access to work. The AI Act lists systems intended to filter applications or assess candidates among high-risk uses. It also sets conditions under which a narrower task with no material influence on a decision may be treated differently. Filing documents and deciding which applications appear on a recruiter’s screen have quite different consequences for the people applying. [2]

Suppliers of high-risk systems have duties that include documenting their design, managing risks, providing for human oversight and assessing conformity. Employers using the systems have duties of their own around operation and monitoring. The European Commission sets out a phased timetable, making the role of the tool and the date both relevant to an individual case. For an applicant, it matters that someone can tell whether an error came from the model or the way an employer used it. [2][3]

Suppose a recruitment team finds that people who took a long career break almost always place near the bottom of the ranking. The recruiter needs to know whether the break was a factor and what data could explain the pattern. Someone needs access to a record of the system’s decisions and the authority to change the way the tool is used. Here, good documentation tells a team why dozens of applications may have had so little chance of reaching a person.

The UK Information Commissioner’s Office published audits of AI recruitment tools in 2024. It issued almost 300 recommendations to providers, including about collecting fewer details, processing data fairly and giving applicants clear information. The audits took place under the UK’s legal framework. For the debate in the EU, they illustrate how many problems can sit inside a system an applicant encounters for just a few minutes. [4]

Human oversight can take several forms. It offers limited protection if a recruiter is handed hundreds of results before lunch and can do little more than approve a suggested queue. Taking responsibility requires time to look at unusual cases, knowledge of the system’s limits and the power to change a result. That has a real cost, even if the software licence is cheap. A buyer assessing the tool needs to consider the conditions people will work in as well as the results of technical tests.

## Who can pay to demonstrate compliance

Consider a team of two engineers. A tool they build might work well in a trial. Bringing it to market involves people, testing and procedures to demonstrate that it meets the rules. The documentation has to remain useful when the underlying data or product changes. A larger company can draw on lawyers and specialists who work across several teams. For a smaller supplier, the same kind of work may use up the time of the people building the product or require help they need to pay for.

The cost of compliance can therefore affect who gets to sell a tool. To measure that effect, somebody would need to follow how many smaller suppliers enter the market and what they actually pay. The AI Act contains forms of support. Small firms, including microenterprises, can provide parts of their technical documentation in a simplified form. The legislation also provides for access to supervised settings where selected systems can be tested. What it takes to use that help will matter as much to a business as the fact that it exists in law. [2][3]

There is another cost that can be hard to put on a page of instructions. When an employer has questions about a model’s treatment of applicants, it may depend on the supplier’s willingness to help. A small competitor with a transparent process might be able to answer quickly. If every buyer asks for evidence in a different format, however, the same team can find itself preparing several packs of documents about the same issue. Procurement requirements, contracts and purchasing habits also shape the price of getting into the market.

Much depends on the institution doing the buying. When it asks competing suppliers for the same kind of evidence, it is easier to compare what each has done. If it asks everyone to follow a lengthy format built around one supplier’s processes, previous knowledge of the paperwork can become an advantage. These choices affect the administrative burden. A procedure that protects an applicant and a file that helps a buyer evaluate a supplier can serve the same goal. It is worth knowing which pages someone will actually read.

## A right people can use

Imagine getting a reply that says only that your application was assessed for suitability. It offers little help if you think the model misread a line of your CV. Depending on the circumstances, rights under the AI Act or data protection law may be relevant. The immediate need is to get an answer from someone who understands the basis of the assessment and can act if it was wrong. [2][4]

Good procedures make it possible to see what a system does in typical and difficult cases. They can also make two suppliers easier to compare when both present their test results clearly. A market where buyers can establish who has the authority to pause a tool when a pattern of mistakes appears is more useful to an applicant than one where the weight of a folder does much of the persuading.

The outcome will depend on the quality of shared requirements and on how people enforce them. An applicant has a stake in finding someone who can take their case seriously. A small developer has a stake in showing that a system is safe without repeating the same work for every buyer. Europe’s approach will also be judged in these everyday encounters. Can the candidate learn why they were turned down, and can a new supplier afford to answer the questions we need answered?
