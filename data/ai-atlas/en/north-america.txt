On 4 January 2024, OpenAI retired text-davinci-003 from its API. The company had announced the withdrawal of older models in advance and suggested replacements. The change fitted on one line of a table. For a developer keeping somebody’s service running, that line might have been the start of a long list. How do you check that the thousands of responses the old model used to deliver in a familiar format still make sense and reach the right people after the switch? [1]

Consider a small company whose software reads maintenance requests. A model separates reports of breakdowns from price enquiries, extracts details and sends its results to staff. A replacement may work smoothly. It may also read colloquial phrases differently or mark more cases as urgent. The invoice shows what the new model costs. The full bill includes time spent checking results, making changes and arranging extra cover. The supplier set the date by which that work had to be done.

## When funding comes back as a bill

A year after the model was withdrawn, the US Federal Trade Commission published a study of partnerships linking three cloud providers, Alphabet, Amazon and Microsoft, with the AI developers Anthropic and OpenAI. Its staff examined agreements and company records. Behind the familiar question of which assistant to use was a more basic one. Who supplies the computing power that allows models to learn and answer customers in the first place? [2]

In some of these partnerships, AI developers committed to spending a large portion of the money they received on computing services from the company providing the funds. An investor helps finance the model, then earns back some of that money through cloud fees. The developer gets access to expensive resources and the cloud company gains a major customer. The question of who has power over the product becomes clearer when the developer wants to move. How many contractual commitments, datasets and tools would have to move with it? [2]

A partnership can speed up development and give a smaller company access to resources it could hardly finance from its own sales. The FTC also identified possible effects on competition when a supplier controls access to computing power or gains sensitive information about its partner’s business. The report examines contractual arrangements and the risks they may create. To see what such dependence might mean further down the market, consider the company with the maintenance software. [2]

A customer may find it hard to tell how independent the firms in a catalogue really are. Two applications can look different while calling the same model or running on the same cloud. A new name on the invoice may solve a customer service problem. To spread the risk of downtime, a buyer needs to know what each service depends on. That matters to anyone who promises customers that the lights will stay on when a supplier has trouble.

## What comes with you when you leave

A business buys access to a remote service. Its application sends data, receives a result and takes another step. Given enough time, decisions accumulate around the way that particular service behaves. Developers get used to the format of the reply. Staff learn which instructions produce consistent results. The owner makes commitments to customers based on familiar response times. Before comparing models, somebody has to find those assumptions and decide which ones really matter.

Models that customers can run themselves may offer more freedom over where to host them. They also put more work on the company choosing that route. Somebody has to maintain the model, manage the cost and check what changes after an update. Another option is to hire a different external supplier. The company still has to compare results on its own cases and establish what happens to data and records from previous sessions.

The UK’s Competition and Markets Authority came at the problem from the cloud market. Its investigation, completed in 2025, examined practical obstacles to moving services or using several cloud providers at once. It considered technical barriers and the cost of moving data. It was a separate study in a different market. It helps explain why moving a model can turn into a larger job involving storage, security and the way information flows through a business. [3]

Think about what happens once something goes wrong. A company may be able to download its forms, invoices and responses. The exported files offer relatively little help in rebuilding the system that worked with them. For the person covering the maintenance desk, a copy of those requests is useful. The urgent question is whether a critical alert will still arrive through the familiar channel when a different model is making the first assessment.

Prices can look similar until someone draws up the move. A business may use one provider for the model, for data storage and for monitoring. If it changes the model and leaves everything else in place, it may have to pay to transfer data and run two sets of contracts. Moving the whole system takes money and time for testing. The ability to leave is itself an investment. A large customer can gain leverage just by being in a position to make that investment.

## Who sets the deadline

During the first month, subscriptions and new features are easy to compare. A year later, it matters whether the business can direct some requests to another model and compare the answers. How quickly could staff take the critical work back by hand? Do they know which decisions took place in the automated service until now? Answering those questions takes time and money, usually for someone whose voice carries little weight in negotiations over access to the model.

A large customer can have a team that watches the calendar of retirements and prepares for them. In a small company, the person reading the announcement might also answer customer complaints and repair the product. They may receive the same public message on the same date. The time they can spare to act on it can be very different. A price change or a withdrawal can therefore distribute costs in ways the manufacturer’s price list barely hints at.

It may take only a few keystrokes to specify another model. Moving decisions built around years of familiar behaviour is a different job. Real choice means a company can make the switch with the money, time and people it actually has. Two suppliers are valuable if the company can move from one to the other and still serve the same clients tomorrow.

The line in OpenAI’s calendar dealt with a single model. It points to a question for anyone building a business on somebody else’s AI. When the supplier makes its next decision, how many decisions will still be yours?
