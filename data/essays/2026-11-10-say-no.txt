### **Mariusz Gorlowski - Author**

November 9 at 4:11 PM

The Day We Stop Saying No

Imagine a world in which no one has taken away our freedom.

You can still make your own decisions. You still have the right to disagree. You can still say no.

Except that, one day, you barely exercise that right any more.

Not because anyone has forced you.

But because the world has begun to function far more smoothly whenever you say yes.

What if this is how one of the greatest changes in human history begins?

Today, we increasingly rely on systems that help us make decisions. A satnav suggests a faster route. Algorithms recommend what we should read or watch. Artificial intelligence can draft messages, analyse information, present arguments and suggest solutions to problems.

There is nothing inherently wrong with this. We develop technology precisely because we want to make our lives easier.

But I would like to draw attention to a particular boundary.

There is a fundamental difference between a system that suggests what we should do and one that begins doing it for us.

Imagine using artificial intelligence at work.

At first, you ask it to draft a reply to a customer. You read the message, change a few sentences and send it.

After a while, you notice that its suggested replies are getting better and better. You rarely need to change anything.

Eventually, you allow the system to respond to some messages on its own.

It is convenient. It saves you time. You can concentrate on something more important.

Then you begin entrusting it with other tasks.

Until one day, you discover that the system is handling most of your correspondence, making small decisions and solving problems you never even knew had arisen.

You can still oversee everything.

But do you?

I am not suggesting that artificial intelligence has suddenly decided to seize control. AI need not be conscious, ambitious or driven by desires of its own for such a change to occur.

All it takes is for us to conclude, more and more often, that its suggestions are better than our own.

And then to start treating those suggestions not as proposals, but as decisions that are hardly worth questioning.

There is a name for this tendency: automation bias.

Researchers have been studying it for decades.

In a 1999 study involving a simulated flight task, Linda Skitka, Kathleen Mosier and Mark Burdick found that participants using an imperfect automated decision aid sometimes followed its recommendations even when those recommendations contradicted their training and other valid information available to them.

The study concerned earlier decision-support systems, not today's generative AI. But the pattern it found remains relevant: trusting a system too readily can make us less likely to check its recommendations.

Now imagine another situation.

You work in an organisation where a system analyses data and recommends particular actions. Approving one of its recommendations takes just a few seconds.

You can reject it, but then you have to explain your decision and accept responsibility if the outcome turns out to be worse.

Formally, the choice is still yours.

In practice, it becomes increasingly tempting simply to click 'Approve'.

And this brings me to a question that matters far more than it first appears:

Is a person truly independent if they are free to make their own decisions, but the entire system around them encourages them not to?

I am not claiming that the future must unfold this way.

Artificial intelligence could bring enormous benefits. It could help us discover things we might never have noticed ourselves, accelerate scientific research and solve problems that have challenged humanity for generations.

I am developing QR FINDER myself, a system that connects physical tags and stickers with a digital platform. I use digital tools, and I can see the extraordinary possibilities that advances in AI are opening up.

I would not want to live in a world where fear of the future leads us to abandon progress.

But neither would I want to live in a world where convenience becomes more important than our ability to think for ourselves.

There is something else worth considering.

Behind many of these systems are people, companies and institutions that establish the rules by which they operate.

So the question of who controls AI is also a question of who sets the terms for everyone who depends on it.

Perhaps the greatest danger will not be the day when artificial intelligence takes away humanity's ability to make decisions.

Perhaps it will be the day when humanity decides, of its own accord, that making decisions is no longer necessary.

In my book The Transition Code, I explore the possibility that humanity represents a transitional stage in the evolution of intelligence.

But before we begin asking whether artificial intelligence might become our successor, perhaps we should ask a more immediate question:

Can we benefit from an intelligence that helps us make better decisions without losing the courage to make our own?

I would like to hear your thoughts.

Where would you draw the line?

In which areas would you allow artificial intelligence to make decisions on your behalf, and where would you always want to have the final say?

Mariusz Gorlowski

Research reference:

Skitka, L. J., Mosier, K. L. & Burdick, M. (1999). “Does automation bias decision-making?” International Journal of Human-Computer Studies, 51(5), 991–1006.
