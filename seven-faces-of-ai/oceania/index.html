Your voice without your consent

Mariusz Gorlowski

In 2017, Te Hiku Media in the far north of Aotearoa New Zealand began transcribing interviews with older speakers of te reo Māori. One aim was to preserve phrases particular to the region and make them easier to find and pass on. There were thousands of hours of digitised audio in the archive. The team could put more recordings online, but searching and transcribing them by hand would take an enormous amount of work. An automatic speech recognition tool grew out of that job.[1]

At first, it sounds simple. A person said something valuable, and a computer could help put the words on a page or screen. A model learning to recognise speech needs examples of people speaking and of how their words are written. Somebody has to decide whose recordings go into that work and what else they may be used for. That is where a question arises which a transcription tool cannot answer. Who gets to decide what happens to a voice once it has been recorded?

Keeping a way of speaking

Te Hiku Media’s account in MAI Journal describes the time it took to transcribe interviews with elders by hand. In 2018 it secured funding for work that began as the Kōrero Māori project. That led to Kaituhi, an automatic transcription tool, and work on synthesised speech in te reo Māori. A community that had gathered the recordings was working towards being able to manage them with tools it had helped create.[1][2]

The scale is worth keeping in mind. In New Zealand’s 2023 Census, 213,849 people reported being able to hold a conversation in te reo Māori. Counting speakers tells us relatively little about how many hours of different varieties have been recorded, who gets to hear them and how much of the sound can be turned into text. In one family it might matter that a young person can hear an older relative say a place name. In another, it may be a story someone understands even though they have few chances to speak the language themselves.[3]

A good transcript makes it easier for people beyond the small group that knows an archive well to find such moments. They can search for a phrase, compare the way people use it and make an extract for learning. A speaker can also correct the transcript if the tool substitutes a more common form for something specific to the region. A model could perform well on average and still miss part of what made a conversation worth recording.

Kaituhi can also transcribe material where te reo Māori and English appear together. That is useful when people go between languages in the same conversation. A tool expecting only one language can lose part of what somebody actually said. For an archive, it is rather like a sentence missing from a transcript. Later users may also lose the trail back to the recording, where they could have heard why a particular word mattered.[2]

One recording can have several uses

Imagine someone recording a conversation with their grandmother, with her permission, so the family has her words and can hear how she said them. Years later, AI can help turn the conversation into text. The same material could also be valuable to somebody developing a model that generates speech in a particular voice. The US Federal Trade Commission has described the use of similar technology to impersonate relatives in scams. There is a question that comes before any such use. What did the person sitting in front of the microphone agree to?[4]

A model doing transcription receives a recording and proposes a written version. A system that synthesises speech can produce audio from text. The result may be a statement the person whose voice it imitates never made. Consent to share one conversation has an audience and a purpose. Permission to use recordings to train another tool is a further decision about access and about what somebody might make from them. When AI can do both tasks, people need to be able to hold that distinction in place.

At Te Hiku Media, one principle informing the work is kaitiakitanga, a form of care and responsibility for something entrusted to you. Under Kaituhi’s terms, stewardship of uploaded data remains with the kaitiaki who submitted it. They can decide how to make material available within an organisation and whether to contribute some of it to improving Papa Reo’s services. The licence also addresses attempts to use the tool to create separate datasets and models. These are concrete decisions about who may use a conversation and for what purpose, including who can change that purpose later.[1][5]

Uploading a recording takes one click. Further questions may take longer to settle. Does someone need to hear the full recording, or will a transcript do? If permission is withdrawn, can copies that others received be removed? What should happen if a person who agreed to preserve a conversation changes their mind about its public use years later? The answers depend on the people involved and on how the material is held. The rules matter from the moment recordings are collected.

Who will notice if a part of the language goes missing

A speech model can pick up patterns that occur most often in its data. It may mistake a local expression for an error and replace it with a more common form. Someone who knows the language can hear the difference immediately. If a tool is built far from the people whose words it handles, its developers may have little idea which errors matter most to them. Control over recordings has a research value as well. It allows people to test how well the system hears those it is meant to serve.

Making some collections available more widely could help other tools come into being. Decisions about access involve people who know where the material came from and understand their obligations to those recorded. Te Hiku Media’s account describes sharing in terms of relationships, purpose and local practice. If an old conversation was recorded for a family, a proposal to use it in a commercial service can be considered on different terms from a relative’s request to hear it.[1]

The original aim of the archive in northern Aotearoa was to preserve words people could return to. AI may help a grandchild find one sentence that was spoken years ago. It may also put a voice into settings the person who made the recording never imagined. Both uses can begin with the same file. What matters is who has the power to decide what happens next.
