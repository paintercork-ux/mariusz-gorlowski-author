What happens to us in a world where technology can do more and more? I am interested not only in what we can create, but also in the choices we make along the way. These are the questions I explore in my books and essays. Alongside my writing, I develop QR FINDER — a practical system that helps people who find lost pets or belongings get in touch with their owners. I enjoy asking difficult questions and looking for practical answers to everyday problems.

Calendar

Yesterday

Our grandfathers and fathers used to tell us about digging foundations with spades, laying bricks by hand and carrying building materials upstairs on their backs. When diggers and cranes arrived on building sites, they took over the heaviest work. Before the digger could get started, though, someone still had to stretch a string between two pegs and show the driver where to dig.

It was much the same with a simple computer program. We decided what it should calculate and gave it a rule to follow. The computer could run that calculation thousands of times, and we still decided what to do with the result.

Today

We tell our children how we used to leaf through encyclopaedias, look things up in libraries and start every piece of writing with a blank sheet of paper. We could spend hours on a single piece, writing out the same page by hand several times over.

Today, AI can suggest an opening line, create an illustration or translate a passage. We still feel we’re at the controls because we type the first instruction. We might ask it to shorten a paragraph and, a moment later, let it decide what matters most. When it can do the work faster and more cheaply, without ever getting tired, it’s easy to accept its version. First we hand over the work, then the decisions, and in the end we may step aside altogether.

Tomorrow

‘In our day, all AI could do was write a bit of text or make a picture.’ We might say that to our children or grandchildren one day, in much the same tone our grandfathers used when they talked about the days before diggers.

We’ve spent generations improving our tools, first to take some of the weight off our backs and later to do some of the thinking for us. The more we leave to machines, the harder it is to see when they start deciding what’s worth doing in the first place.

If our children look back on today’s AI as a toy that could piece together words and pictures, what exactly will their machines be doing for them? Perhaps the machines will come up with their own questions and get to work before anyone has even thought to ask.

What will be left of us then?
