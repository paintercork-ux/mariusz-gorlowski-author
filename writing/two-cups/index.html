Two Cups

Mariusz Gorlowski

‘Dad, I must have read your message ten times.’

Marek reached for the teapot. Lena slid her cup towards him without thinking.

‘Black tea with orange?’ he asked.

‘I think you’ve already made it.’

He’d made a pot of plain black tea as well. At the shop that morning, he’d tried to remember whether the one she liked had orange peel in it or bergamot. The woman behind the counter had suggested fifty grams of each. He’d bought a hundred of each. He usually bought tea bags by the box and only thought about how many were left when he could see the bottom.

Lena kept her coat on until he told her she could hang it over her chair. She’d left her bag in the hall. It was small and stiff, with a worn handle. She’d come by train and had a ticket back that evening.

‘You know what I did when it came through?’ she said. ‘I went out on the balcony. In my socks. It was after eleven and raining. I thought I’d read it again in the morning and find I’d made half of it up.’

Marek pushed the sugar bowl to the middle of the table. She used to take sugar. He picked up a teaspoon to break up the lumps, then set it down again.

He’d known since Tuesday that Lena was coming. A suggested calendar entry had appeared on his phone just above a reminder about physio. Saturday at five. Lena. He tapped Confirm. She wanted to see him at last. On Wednesday he washed the windows, and on Thursday he shifted the table so her chair leg would sit clear of the gap between the tiles. He’d been checking the time all day. When she buzzed from downstairs, he got to the door so fast he knocked his bad wrist against the frame.

This was the first he’d heard about a message.

‘Have you got it there?’ he asked.

Her smile faltered.

‘Of course. Hang on.’

She took out her phone, tapped his name and scrolled. Marek began counting the flicks of her thumb, but she was going too fast for him to keep up. On his own phone, the conversation with Lena had spent weeks among the threads marked as dealt with. He’d sometimes catch a notice that she’d sent a photo or written something about work. Since the row about her drawings, nearly a year ago, there had been whole weeks when the sight of her name was enough to make him put the phone down.

Lena found the message and turned the screen towards him. The first thing he noticed was the time. 11.14 pm. He’d been asleep on the sofa by then on Monday. After midnight, the dishwasher had clicked off and woken him. He remembered having to straighten his bad arm with his other hand.

He read the message.

‘I said you could come on Sunday. On Friday I threw your portfolio out with the rest of the paper. Then I said that if it mattered to you, you’d have come for it sooner. I remember exactly what happened. I’m sorry about the drawings and about what I said to you. If you still feel like coming round, I’ll make you that orange tea at five on Saturday.’

Every detail was where it belonged. Especially Sunday.

* * *

The red portfolio had two black elastic bands and a handle he’d made out of string. He remembered the day Lena brought it into the workshop. She was sixteen, and she wanted to draw his hands while he sharpened a chisel. She dragged her stool so close it brushed his boot.

‘Could you at least wait till I’m done?’

‘Then you’ll put your hands where you think they should go. They’re right where I want them now.’

She drew the hand dark with dust and left a wedge of bare paper between his thumb and forefinger. Looking over her shoulder, Marek saw for the first time how hard he pressed his thumb into the handle. Later, when his forearm began to ache, he’d remember the drawing and ease his grip.

The portfolio grew heavier over the years. There were drawings of chairs Lena had measured after school and sketches of old furniture left out on the pavement for collection. On one sheet she’d drawn the hand of a woman on a bus, in profile and life-size. Marek asked if she’d bought a ticket for the drawing pad, seeing as it needed a seat of its own. Lena told him to guess what the woman was holding in her other hand. It turned out to be a small plastic butter tub with a canary inside, on its way to the vet. Lena never drew the bird. She remembered the holes punched through the lid.

When Marek gave up the lease on his workshop, Lena asked him to put the portfolio by the door. She was doing up her flat across town and most of what she owned was in boxes. The portfolio was too big to pack, so she arranged to borrow a friend’s car and collect it separately. She sent him a photo of it and asked if Sunday would work. He texted back that any time after lunch was fine. He’d already locked up when he went back inside to take it down from the top shelf and leave it by the door.

On Friday the man from the clearance company rang. He had a van and a crew free nearby, but Sunday would cost more. The landlord had said he could get the floor painted before Monday if he got the keys early. Marek said yes. He liked the thought of going home that evening with an empty boot. Come Monday, nobody would have any reason to call him about the workshop.

The crew told him they took paper separately. He saw the red corner sticking out beneath a stack of drawer templates. All he had to do was bend down, pick up the portfolio and put it in the car. He remembered the two elastic bands. One had slipped halfway off. He looked at the pile and pushed it towards the door with his boot.

‘This lot as well?’ asked the lad in the high-vis jacket.

‘That lot too.’

On Sunday Lena sent him a photo of the empty workshop. Through the glass in the door he could see pale rectangles on the wall where the shelving had been. She was still in the car park with her friend’s car.

He rang her and went straight to anything that sounded helpful. Did she have pictures of the drawings? Had she looked on her phone? He’d print anything she could find. When she asked why he’d agreed to Sunday then, he told her that if she cared that much about the portfolio she’d have taken it home ages ago.

She hung up and went to give the car back. For a long time they both remembered the sixty zlotys she’d spent on petrol. Whenever they tried to talk about the workshop, that was the detail Marek thought of first. He could pay her back sixty zlotys right there and be done.

A month after he cleared out the workshop, he went round to see his sister. Irena had seen Lena a few days earlier and asked about the portfolio. He said Lena had left some papers behind when she moved. He insisted he’d reminded her twice to collect them. Irena sighed about young people doing six things at once and losing track. Then Marek showed her a picture of the empty workshop. She looked at the photo and said at least he’d got that sorted.

Now Lena was sitting across the table, holding her cup carefully by the handle. She’d taken her first sip while he was still reading. He could hand her back the phone and say he remembered writing the message. Her face would brighten again. He’d wanted that from the moment she came into the kitchen.

He slid the sugar bowl to one side. The window was a few feet from the table. He could get up, open it a crack and give himself a few seconds to think of something to say.

‘An AI assistant on my phone wrote that message,’ he said. ‘I let it send replies on its own. I’ve only just seen what it said.’

Lena held the cup where it was. She put her free hand on the saucer before she set it down. The porcelain rang against it.

* * *

‘Just this one?’ she asked.

‘I think there are quite a few.’

‘How many?’

Marek reached for his phone. With his thumb still stiff, he had to switch hands to unlock it. Lena watched him do it with the concentration of someone waiting to see another person finish signing their name.

For six weeks after he broke his wrist he’d dictated nearly everything. He got quite good at it. By the time he took his jacket off he could get down a shopping list and find out when his next appointment was. The assistant was better at making out the names of his tablets than Irena was. One evening it drafted replies to fifteen messages he hadn’t got round to answering. He read through the lot and sent them in a few minutes. When he started getting home later from physio, he let it send the simple replies without asking. Later, he extended that to most messages from family and close friends. When the assistant asked about Lena, he stopped for a moment at her name. Then he turned it on for her too.

‘You hardly ever rang me,’ he said.

He wanted to take the words back as soon as he heard them. It sounded as though he’d found a way to make it her fault.

‘I tried twice,’ she said. ‘In August. The second time you texted back, “I’m glad you texted.” I thought maybe that was easier for you.’

They went back to August. His phone put a little grey symbol next to the messages the assistant had sent. Lena drew the phone closer, then set it down again less than a minute later. The grey marks were next to almost every reply. When the screen dimmed, Marek touched it to keep it on.

‘I was sure you’d remember the day of the interview,’ she said.

‘What did you tell me?’

‘I was sitting outside the furniture restorer’s. I’d got there too early. I could hear her moving a chair inside. It felt like if I got up and walked through that door I’d have started something I couldn’t back out of. I’d already put the interview off once.’

Marek knew the place. A year earlier he’d driven Lena there to get another look at an armchair with a curved back in the window. Standing on the pavement, he’d gone on at some length about the thickness of the varnish. The woman inside heard every word through the open window above the door. Lena pulled at his sleeve until he let it go and they walked on.

‘What did I say back?’

‘“You’ve still got five minutes. Sit for a bit. Tell me how it went when you come out.”’

Marek looked down. He could have said that. If he’d seen her message in time, he’d have been pleased to think of it. He knew exactly how much of the interview Lena could get through in her head in the five minutes before she went in.

‘I went in,’ she said. ‘I sent you a photo afterwards. The bench where I work now.’

He took his hand off the screen. He’d seen that day’s photos in a Sunday round-up, sitting in the clinic with a numbered ticket in one hand. He remembered a chip in the pale edge of a workbench. He’d assumed Lena was showing him a piece of work she wanted his opinion on.

‘Can I see it again?’ he asked.

‘Another time.’

For a while they could hear somebody upstairs dragging a heavy vacuum cleaner across a rug. There was a diagonal streak of chalk near the pocket of Lena’s black trousers. He’d noticed it when she arrived and been pleased to think she still worked some Saturdays. She must have come straight from a job. He nearly asked about the chalk mark. He had enough questions now to last all evening. He waited.

‘Dad, can you stop it reading anything else I send you?’

‘Yes.’

He went into the settings, turned the assistant off in his messages and laid the phone down facing her. Lena read what it said and pushed it back to him. She sat up and looked straight at him as though the phone were something they’d finally thought to move out of the way. Then she slipped her own phone into her pocket.

‘On Monday I wrote to you about the portfolio. I went through what happened from the start. I asked if we could finally talk about it. I’d mentioned picking up the orange tea on my way home. That’s how it knew.’

Marek nodded. At six the following morning he’d got the round-up with the visit already arranged. The invitation and her reply had both gone through while he was asleep on the sofa. Over breakfast he’d looked up her train and thought that was all he needed.

‘Could you show me what you wrote on Monday?’ he asked.

Lena thought about it. Then she took her phone out and nodded.

He kept coming back to one line. ‘I wanted you to ask me what was in it.’ Underneath she’d listed the places she’d tried, looking for somebody who might remember the work. She’d got hold of her art teacher and written to the photographer from her end-of-school exhibition. She’d even rung the sorting centre to find out how long they kept waste paper, though she knew too many days had passed. While she did all that, he’d been telling Irena how many times he’d reminded Lena to collect it.

He gave her back her phone.

‘I saw the portfolio on Friday. I had my car keys on me. It would have fitted in the boot.’

Lena pushed her chair back a little, its legs scraping across the tiles. To Marek it was the loudest sound in the flat.

She stood up to get a napkin. They used to keep them in the drawer below the cutlery. She looked there and closed it again.

‘Behind the bread bin,’ he said.

He reached across the table for the empty pot. Lena looked at his hand and he drew it back. He’d been moving things around for half an hour. His hands could stay on his knees for a bit, awkward as that felt at the kitchen table.

‘I told the lad to take it,’ he said. ‘I wanted the workshop empty. If I’d kept it, I’d have had to ring you and explain why I’d cleared out early and arrange to meet you somewhere else. I felt like I couldn’t take on one more thing. So I left you to deal with the whole lot on Sunday.’

Lena came back with two napkins. She set one beside her cup and passed the other to him.

‘For ages I thought if I’d started that call differently we might have looked for it together. If I’d said quietly that my first drawings from school were in there, you might have asked me where I was. You might have come.’

‘I was waiting for you to stop asking me about it,’ he said. ‘I’d have been happier if all you wanted was the petrol money.’

‘I know. You offered it three times.’

She folded the napkin in half. There was a triangle of dark varnish under one of her nails. Marek knew the shape. He’d spent years getting marks like that, working on the edges of furniture. Once, if she’d asked him about it, he’d have spent ten minutes showing her how to get a brush into a corner.

‘You’ve every right to be furious with me,’ he said. ‘For Friday and for what I went round saying about you afterwards. I’m sorry, Lena.’

‘Say what you told them.’

‘I lied to Irena. I said I’d reminded you to collect it. I knew full well what day we’d agreed.’

Lena sat down and leaned back in her chair as if she were finding out for the first time that she could sit comfortably in it.

‘Auntie Irena gave me a memory card for my birthday,’ she said. ‘“Somewhere to keep the important things.” I remember thinking she must have gone to the shop specially on her way home from work.’

Marek pictured Irena at the till with her shopping bag on wheels. She probably meant well. Lena had had to take the present and thank her, then go on making tea for everyone as if she were grateful that one more person had found a way to help her keep track of her things.

‘I’ll ring her today,’ he said. ‘I’ll tell her what I did.’

Lena wrapped both hands around her cup.

‘Tell her whatever you need to about the portfolio. If I want her to know about the interview or anything else I wrote, I’ll tell her myself.’

‘All right.’

‘And please don’t ask her to ring me for you. I’ve had enough of people speaking for one another today.’

He thought of all the times he’d asked Irena to find out how Lena was doing. She could pass on a recipe or remind her about somebody’s name day. He kept that to himself. He asked if she wanted more tea. She pointed to the same pot and he filled her cup halfway.

‘I really wanted to hear from you that night,’ she said. ‘I wrote the message before I left the workshop. I walked home and took my phone out twice to change things. When you said five on Saturday, the first thing I did was check I had a clean shirt. Then I looked up the trains.’

Marek moved the teapot away from the edge of the table. Lena was wearing a pale blue shirt. The cuffs had been carefully ironed, and the corner of her ticket stuck out of the pocket. He’d seen her wear that shirt in photos from exhibitions. He’d almost teased her for dressing up to see her own father.

‘I’m glad you came,’ he said.

She turned the cup until her thumb settled comfortably against the handle. She drank slowly, and Marek picked up his own cup. Before they finished she asked how much longer he had to go to physio. He showed her where the movement caught. To turn his wrist he had to support his elbow with his other hand. She watched closely. Then she pointed out where he used to put his thumb when he was sharpening the chisel.

When she looked up at the clock, she got to her feet and put on her coat. Marek handed her the bag. It was light. Something rustled inside as he picked it up.

‘I brought you some of that tea,’ she said. ‘I’ll leave it here. You’ve got enough for the winter now.’

She took out a paper packet, crumpled at the corners, and set it beside his keys. Marek asked if he could walk her to the tram. She said yes, and they walked side by side past two junctions. At the shelter they stopped by the route map, barely visible behind the misted glass. Lena cleared the name of the stop with her sleeve. Marek began telling her about a man from the next workshop who used to paint the destinations on the signs at the front of the trams. He broke off when they heard a tram coming round the bend.

‘Go on,’ Lena said.

He had time to tell her that the man used to leave a tiny dot on the right-hand side of every sign. Just for himself. She asked if any of them were still around. He showed her the terminus on the map and turned back towards the tracks. When the doors opened, Lena rested her hand on his shoulder for a moment.

‘Thanks for telling me,’ she said, and stepped aboard.

Marek waited until the tram had passed the junction. He was holding the ticket he’d bought for himself while she looked at the timetable. He thought she might want him to go with her to the station. He slipped it into his pocket. Outside his flat he took out his phone and rang Irena.

* * *

She answered on the third ring. He heard a knife knock against a chopping board and the rustle of a tea towel. She said she was about to fry some celeriac fritters, so he offered to call back in an hour.

‘Tell me now,’ she said. ‘I can take the pan off the heat.’

Marek stood in the hall where Lena’s bag had been. It might have been easier to say it if he went into the other room and walked up and down. He stayed under the coat hooks. He told Irena they’d agreed on Sunday, then described the elastic bands on the portfolio and the van that came on Friday. He had to go back to the first sentence. Irena took the pause after it to mean he’d finished.

‘Marek, you told me you’d kept reminding her right up to the last minute.’

‘I did say that. I told her today I’d made it up.’

He heard the chopping board clack as Irena tipped the peelings into the bin. He waited for her to bring the phone back to her ear.

‘I meant that card to be from you, you know,’ she said. ‘I bought it with some of the money you sent for the cake. I thought you’d be pleased.’

Marek remembered sending the money and wishing Lena a happy birthday. His daughter had got advice in his name on how to look after the very things he’d thrown away. He nearly said a memory card would always come in handy. He took the tram ticket from his pocket and folded it in two.

‘I should have told you the truth before the party,’ he said. ‘If you told anyone else, will you help me put it right?’

‘Only Jurek. He’s in the next room. I’ll tell him when he comes in for dinner.’

She asked what had happened to the paper after they took it. From Lena’s message Marek knew it had gone from the sorting centre long ago. He told Irena the portfolio was gone. Once he’d said it aloud, he stopped thinking about where he might still look.

Before they hung up she asked if the drawing of his hand on the chisel had been in the portfolio too. He’d sent her a picture of it once, just after showing her a kitchen cupboard he’d repaired. She’d kept the conversation for the address of the shop where he’d got the handles. She said she’d look for it in the week.

Marek texted Lena that he’d spoken to Irena and that she’d tell Jurek what had happened. He tapped out three sentences with one finger. He’d typed an ‘l’ where there should have been an ‘ł’. He fixed it, read the whole thing through and sent it. At half past nine, when she was home, Lena replied. ‘Thank you for ringing her.’ A little later she asked him to send the photo if Irena found it.

Irena found it on Wednesday. The drawing was lying on a workbench under a strip of wood, with the head of a screw beside it and an elastic band showing at the edge of the shot. He could see the crease where Lena had tried to pick up the sheet with a wet finger. There was the wedge of light between the fingers, too. Marek saved the photo. He thought about cropping out the shadow of the wood so the drawing would fill the frame. He sent Lena the whole picture.

‘I remember that smell,’ Lena wrote. ‘You were always spilling linseed oil there.’

A minute later she wrote that she’d like to use the picture at the workshop’s open day. Her boss had asked everyone to bring something they’d made or drawn when they were starting out and put it alongside whatever they were working on now. She could find a spot for the photo near the armchair she was restoring. Marek read the message twice. Then he asked if he could come.

‘In three weeks. Saturday at five. You could give me a hand an hour before, if you’re free.’

He took the calendar with his physio appointments out of the drawer. He usually took his neighbour shopping at four on Saturdays. He went over that evening and they agreed to do the shopping in the morning that Saturday. When he got home, he texted Lena to say he’d be at the workshop at four.

* * *

He got there ten minutes early. In the window a little print was propped against the pale back of a chair. Cupping his hand to the glass to block the reflection of the street, he could make out the screw and the elastic band. Lena came and opened the door.

‘There are chairs upstairs,’ she said. ‘I’d like to put a few by the entrance for anyone who wants to sit down. Can you help me carry five?’

They brought four down straight away, but the fifth had a loose leg. Marek grabbed it by the back and lifted it without thinking. Lena asked him to hold it while she checked the joint. At her suggestion he moved his hand lower. Then she stuck a note on the seat that said ‘For repair’ and they put it aside. She borrowed the last chair from the office.

Lena’s boss came to join them. Marek recognised the woman from the day he’d stood outside going on about the varnish. He wanted to tell her he might have judged it too harshly with the sun on the window. Lena introduced him by name. The first visitors were at the door. Would he mind taking their coats? Marek spent the first hour handing out hangers. Someone even gave him a bike key to mind. He heard Lena showing visitors how they could feel the tool marks under the armrest. An older woman stopped beside Lena’s drawing, her hand held exactly as his had been all those years ago.

When there were fewer people about, Lena asked him to come and look at the armrest. There was a shallow dip in the wood, easy to miss until he ran his thumb over it. A hand had worn that hollow over years. She’d left the hollow there, she said. When the owner got their chair back, their hand would still settle in the same place.

‘I’m glad you came,’ she said, looking at the wood.

He recognised the turn of phrase. He’d seen words much like those in one of the messages sent in his name. He wanted to come back with something just as neat. Then he realised she was happy to wait while he thought.

‘I’m glad I got to see you work,’ he said. ‘You’ve got everything to hand here. I used to walk halfway round my workshop to find a clamp.’

‘Your clamp was always above the door.’

‘The little one.’

‘The big one too. I used to move it when it got in the way of the light.’

It was only when she laughed that he noticed how many people could hear them. The day was nearly over. Lena’s boss was brushing grey marks from the mat and the older woman had sat down on one of the chairs they’d brought down. Lena leaned against the workbench in just the spot she’d photographed for him after the interview. Marek ran a finger over the pale, chipped edge. This time he knew why he wanted to remember it.

* * *

By spring they were having tea more often than either of them had expected on that Saturday in November. Sometimes Lena cancelled because she had a piece to finish or was going to the cinema. Sometimes Marek asked for a different time. When they both had an afternoon free, he’d pick up two slices of poppy-seed roll on his way to hers. Lena showed him where to cut the wrapper so the crumbs didn’t spill into the bag. He gave up telling her where to start cutting the cake.

They sent each other short messages. When Marek’s wrist stiffened he dictated them to his phone. One day in March he dictated a text about the tomatoes he was starting on the windowsill. ‘You’ll have a few new seedlings by Easter.’ Half an hour later he saw what his phone had sent. ‘You’ll have a few new siblings by Easter.’ He rang before Lena had time to finish her reply. They had a laugh about the plans his phone had made for the family. He still checked every word before he sent anything important. Dictating made things easier. What to say to Lena was up to him.

One afternoon she asked if she could come round after work with something to read. At half past five he put the kettle on. She’d asked for stronger tea the previous Sunday, so he put an extra spoonful in the pot. When she came in, she had a flat portfolio under her arm. She’d picked it up from the bookbinder on the way. She showed him the grey cloth, the stitching along the fold and the piece of red ribbon she’d chosen to tie it shut.

‘Can I put it on the table?’ she asked.

He moved the teapot to the sideboard to give her room. Lena untied the ribbon. The photograph of his hand on the chisel was on top. Under it were a few sheets of studies of chair arms and a new sketch of a woman’s hand gripping a rail on a bus. One of the sheets had picked up a faint smudge of graphite from the next, so Lena slipped a fresh piece of tissue between them. Then she took a book out of her bag and handed him another that he’d lent her years ago. She kicked her shoes off and tucked one leg under her on the chair, the way she used to sit when she did her homework at his place.

‘Are you taking the portfolio with you today?’ Marek asked.

‘Tomorrow. After work. Does that suit?’

He could have gone to pick up some window glass then, but the shop was open on Saturdays too. He checked the hours on the slip beside the kettle.

‘I’ll be home from five,’ he said. ‘I’ll leave it on the chest of drawers in the hall.’

At five the next day the portfolio was on the chest. Seven minutes later he heard Lena’s footsteps on the stairs. She hung her keys on the hook he’d always kept free for her, slipped off her shoes and put the portfolio in her bag. Then she fetched her book and sat down at the table.

Marek set down two cups. Lena opened her book where she’d turned down a corner the day before. She read and he sat across from her. Her trust had come back in ordinary moments like this, when she could take him at his word and get on with her day knowing he’d do what he’d said. Marek picked up the teapot. He poured his first and gave hers another minute.
