What If Someone Who's Gone Called You?

Mariusz Gorlowski

Imagine a family in which two school-age children lose their mother.

They are left with their father, who is struggling to come to terms with his wife's death.

In the evenings, the younger child asks when Mum will come back. The older one talks less and less about their day. Their father tries to be strong for them all, though he can barely cope himself.

All they have left are photographs, family videos and voice messages.

Using these, an artificial intelligence system can recreate the woman's voice and face, and even hold conversations resembling those they had before she died.

The father decides to give it a try.

Not for himself. For the children.

At first, they look at the screen uncertainly. Then they begin talking about school, friends and small achievements. They hear the familiar voice. They can listen to a bedtime story again.

The evenings become calmer.

In this imagined story, the children gradually return to playing, spending time with friends and their everyday routines. They do not forget their mother. But they begin to learn how to live with her absence.

Their father sees the change and feels relieved.

One evening, after the children have fallen asleep, he opens the system himself.

He just wants to hear his wife's voice for a moment.

The conversation lasts five minutes.

The following evening, a little longer.

Then it becomes a daily ritual.

Months pass.

The children ask less and less often to speak to their digital mother.

Their father, however, turns to it more and more.

He turns down invitations from friends. Puts off meeting people. He prefers to stay at home, where he can hear that familiar laugh and talk about everything that happened during his day.

The system always has time for him.

Until one evening, one of the children says:

"Dad, we don't need to talk to Mum tonight."

The father replies:

"But I do."

The same technology that helped the children move forward has begun to hold their father in the past.

Not because the system has changed.

What has changed is what the people using it need from it.

This is a fictional story. But the technology that could make it possible already exists.

In 2024, University of Cambridge researchers Tomasz Hollanek and Katarzyna Nowaczyk-Basińska described the risks associated with digitally recreating the dead. Among other concerns, they discussed the emotional consequences of such relationships and the difficulties that may arise when people try to end them.

Their research does not prove that such technology helps children or harms adults. It does, however, make clear how many questions remain unanswered, particularly when grieving people and children are involved.

Could this be part of why we find it so difficult to agree about AI?

Some see it as a lifeline. Others see it as a threat.

But does either side have to be wrong?

Perhaps we spend too much time asking whether artificial intelligence is good or bad, instead of considering who it helps, under what circumstances, and what consequences it might bring.

Does something that helps a person today necessarily remain good for them tomorrow?

For the children, their mother's voice became a bridge back to everyday life. For their father, it became a refuge from the present.

One family. One loss. One system. Two different outcomes.

Now imagine that your phone rings tomorrow.

On the screen is the name of someone who is no longer here.

You know it is artificial intelligence.

You also know that, on the other end, you will hear a familiar voice.

Would you press the green button or the red one?

Mariusz Gorlowski

Research reference:

Hollanek, T. & Nowaczyk-Basińska, K. (2024). Griefbots, Deadbots, Postmortem Avatars: on Responsible Applications of Generative AI in the Digital Afterlife Industry. Philosophy & Technology, 37, Article 63.
